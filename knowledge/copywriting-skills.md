# Copywriting skills

Ore's copywriting skills, one per heading. They were built from 8 books (Masterson's Copywriting Secrets and Great Leads, Take Their Money, The Brain Audit, Influence, Predictably Irrational, StoryBrand, Halbert's editing formula) and merged into one skill wherever the books teach the same thing. The full book notes live in `knowledge/books/` for reference. Each skill says what it is, how to use it in a 15-60s video or post, and where it came from.

Use them the way a good copywriter does: pick the few that fit this brand, audience and awareness stage. Don't stuff every skill into one script.

## A. Before writing

### 1. Find the one big idea
- One idea, one emotion, one story, one benefit, one next step per script. Points that compete pull attention apart.
- Write the idea as one sentence above the script. If it takes a paragraph, there's no idea yet.
- Dinner-party test: could the viewer repeat it in one phrase tonight? It must be big, easy to grasp, clearly about the viewer and **easy to believe**.
- Make it new, not "better". "A better X" still gets filed as X, and filed means skipped.
- *From: Masterson, Great Leads, Take Their Money.*

### 2. Find the real want (the core complex)
- Ask what the viewer really wants, and don't stop at the first answer. "Make money" is really "quit the job and prove everyone wrong". The deepest motive is usually status, being made whole, or proving something to themselves.
- Sell an identity: from who they are now to who they want to be ("not the guy who hits snooze five times anymore").
- If drafts keep needing rewrites, the real want hasn't been found yet. Go back to the research.
- *From: Masterson, StoryBrand.*

### 3. Read the awareness stage first
- Ask "what does the viewer already know?" before writing. It decides the opening.
- Most aware: give the deal. Product aware: prove we're the best pick. Solution aware: show we get them the result they want. Problem aware: show you feel the pain before any product. Unaware: hold attention with no sign of a pitch.
- The less aware or more jaded the viewer, the more indirect the opening (story, bold claim, secret). The more aware, the more direct (offer, promise).
- Jaded markets that have seen every pitch get treated as less aware.
- *From: Great Leads, RMBC.*

### 4. Cast the viewer as the hero
- The viewer is the hero; the creator or brand is the guide. A creator's own struggle shows they've been there; it never turns into their victory lap.
- Talk about yourself in the low moments. When it turns good, the win belongs to the viewer.
- Five-second test: can the viewer tell what this is, how it helps them and what to do next? If you confuse, you lose.
- *From: StoryBrand, Halbert.*

## B. The problem

### 5. Open on one problem
- The brain notices threats before rewards. Open on the problem, not the benefit.
- Isolate one problem for one person. "Allergic to cats?" stops the right viewer; "Got allergies?" stops no one.
- If the viewer can't name the cause, name the moment they feel it ("Phone in your hand before your eyes are even open?").
- Sell one thing per video. Other problems and offers get their own videos.
- *From: Brain Audit.*

### 6. Raise the stakes
- Turn the irritant into a crisis: follow the problem line with what happens if they leave it ("and that's how another year goes by").
- People work harder to avoid a loss than to get a gain. Frame the cost of not acting as a loss.
- Fear is salt: one or two stake lines, then hope. Lead with fear only for protection products.
- *From: Brain Audit, StoryBrand, Influence, Masterson.*

### 7. Name the villain, at three levels
- A villain is the root cause the viewer already hates (the app that steals your mornings, the job that eats your life). One per script, real, never invented.
- Hit three levels, one line each: external (what's visibly wrong), internal (how it feels; this is what people pay to fix), philosophical (why it isn't fair: "you shouldn't have to...").
- The "not your fault" angle: the problem got worse because of an outside force.
- *From: StoryBrand, RMBC.*

### 8. Make their current way the problem
- A problem can appear just because something better exists. Show the better state and the old way becomes the problem: "You're still doing X while people like you are doing Y."
- *From: Brain Audit.*

### 9. Sell to the hot state
- Calm people underrate how badly they want the fix. Put the viewer back in the moment the problem hurts most (the 6am alarm, the empty bank app) before the offer.
- For problem aware viewers, find that point of maximum anxiety and name it.
- *From: Predictably Irrational, Great Leads.*

## C. Hooks and the first seconds

### 10. Write the hook (the 4 U's)
- The hook is the first spoken line, frame 0 and the on-screen text working as one unit.
- It needs at least two of: **unique** (something they haven't seen in the feed; non-negotiable), **useful** (what's in it for them; curiosity alone doesn't hold), **ultra-specific** (odd, exact numbers and details feel real), **urgent** (why it matters now).
- Keep it short: most top hooks are eight words or fewer.
- Breakthrough hooks put into words what the audience already thinks or fears right now.
- Check competitors' hooks so ours don't sound like theirs.
- *From: Take Their Money, Masterson.*

### 11. Pick the opening type
Six openings, from most direct to most indirect:
- **Offer:** the deal and guarantee up front, plus the reason why ("we're doing this because..."). Answer "what's the catch?" before they ask.
- **Promise:** the one biggest result as the hook, with proof by second 3. Big, specific and believable at once.
- **Problem-solution:** the creator inside the worst moment of the problem, "I get it", then the turn.
- **Secret:** a named but unrevealed method or cause ("the thing nobody tells you about X"). Show what's in the bag; never open it in the lead.
- **Proclamation:** one bold, true claim or prediction, proved within seconds and bridged to the product by second 10.
- **Story:** start mid-scene with a hero the viewer recognizes and a contrast up front. The promise lands by second 8-10.
- Cold traffic and organic lean indirect (story, proclamation, secret). Retargeting and warm audiences lean direct (offer, promise).
- *From: Great Leads.*

### 12. Do the five jobs of the first 10 seconds
1. Stop the scroll with something they haven't seen.
2. Add a detail that builds on the hook without giving the secret away.
3. One line of credibility.
4. The big promise, as early as possible.
5. Open a loop ("in a second I'll show you...").
- By the end of the opening, everything that would make them want it has been said. The rest proves it.
- *From: Take Their Money, Masterson.*

### 13. Tell a story
- Stories get past the viewer's guard: they stop being critical when they're entertained.
- Start in the middle of the conflict, with a place and a time. Give a hero the viewer sees themselves in, raise the stakes, keep the climb short, end on a payoff.
- The underdog arc is a go-to: an ordinary person tries something, people doubt or mock them, they prove everyone wrong. It hits two deep desires, approval and vindication.
- *From: Masterson, Great Leads.*

## D. The middle

### 14. Name the mechanism
- They've tried other fixes that failed. Explain why: the cause they didn't know about (UMP), and why our way works where the others didn't (UMS).
- Give it a name. A named method or system feels new and real, and nobody else has it.
- Explain it simply enough that two tipsy friends could repeat it at a bar.
- *From: RMBC, Great Leads, Take Their Money.*

### 15. Pull the four big emotions (new, easy, safe, big)
- **New**, amplified by **only**: this is a breakthrough and only we have it. New gives hope that this time it will work.
- **Easy**, amplified by **anybody**: few steps, little time, works even for people who think they're bad at this. "Not" lines remove barriers: "You don't need experience, money, or to show your face."
- **Safe**, amplified by **predictable**: show the pattern, the track record, the people it already worked for. A believable exact number ("93%") beats "100%".
- **Big**, amplified by **fast**: the size of the change, and how soon it comes.
- Every line should carry at least one. Most scripts lean on two or three.
- *From: Take Their Money.*

### 16. Answer each doubt before it forms
- Run objection, claim, proof, benefit for each "yeah, but". You don't have to say the objection out loud; the claim answers it.
- Get the doubts from the four emotions: Why haven't I heard of this? Can I do it? Does it work? How much, how fast? Plus: why you, why now, what if it fails?
- Move the answers up: credentials before the claim, "it's simple" before it sounds hard, the guarantee before the price.
- Objections handled as dialogue read naturally on camera.
- *From: Take Their Money, Halbert, Masterson.*

### 17. Prove it
- Three kinds: factual (numbers, sources), anecdotal (stories that show), social (people like them).
- Show, don't tell: screenshots, results on screen, a demo. Build to the strongest proof last.
- Reframe each proof so it lands (per year instead of per month; compared with something familiar).
- Real proof only. A faked number or testimonial burns the trust every other skill depends on.
- *From: Masterson, Take Their Money, Influence.*

### 18. Be the guide: empathy plus authority
- Empathy: say you get the internal problem ("I know what it's like to...", "I was so sick of..."). Similarity is a shortcut to trust.
- Authority: one or two proof beats, such as a result, a number or a credential. Piling them on makes the guide the hero again.
- Admit one small flaw ("it's not the cheapest") so the big claims land. Seeming to argue against your own interest is the strongest authority.
- *From: StoryBrand, Influence.*

### 19. Give a plan
- Turn "how it works" into three steps, never more than six, and name it ("the 3-minute reset").
- Add a few promises that kill fears (cancel any time, no face needed).
- Make the first step tiny. People repeat their own first choice.
- *From: StoryBrand, Predictably Irrational.*

### 20. Paint the success
- Show the specific after, not a vague better life: the after shot, the moment, the life.
- Use the deepest private wants: status, revenge on the doubters, ease, pride, being envied. Stories beat raw data.
- Set the expectation before the demo ("watch how fast this is"); what they expect is what they experience. Save any weakness for after.
- Get them to picture owning it ("your morning, your setup").
- *From: StoryBrand, Take Their Money, Predictably Irrational.*

### 21. Keep the golden thread
- The product is at one end of the thread and the viewer's heart is at the other. Every line connects to both.
- An entertaining video that leaves people unsure what was advertised has failed. Keep the product in the story.
- Link the feeling the hook creates to the feeling at the close.
- *From: Masterson.*

### 22. Leave no safe place to stop
- The viewer is always looking for a place to swipe. Never give them one.
- Mix three tools: cliffhangers ("and then it got worse"), lines that leave the story open ("When the results came in, I was crushed."), and high notes that make them want the next one.
- Open the next loop just before closing the last one. Put the punch at the end of each line.
- *From: Halbert, Take Their Money.*

## E. Persuasion levers

### 23. Contrast and anchoring
- What comes first sets the scale. Show the expensive or painful option first, then ours. Show the bad "before", then the "after".
- The first number seen anchors every price after it. Name the costly alternative (a coach, a clinic, a wasted year) before the price.
- Name a fresh category so the viewer can't price you as the cheap old thing.
- *From: Influence, Predictably Irrational.*

### 24. Give first
- People repay what they're given. Give real value in the video (the tip, the method, the free tool) before any ask.
- Ask big, then step down to the real ask: "I was going to charge $500. Then I made it free." The first ask has to be believable.
- *From: Influence, Masterson.*

### 25. Get a small yes
- A small yes leads to a big one. Ask a question the viewer answers in their head ("You've tried this and quit, right?").
- Public, chosen acts stick: comment a keyword, comment their goal, save.
- Label the viewer with the identity you want them to live up to ("If you're still watching, you're not like most people").
- *From: Influence.*

### 26. Show people like them
- When unsure, people copy others, most of all others like them. Show creators and users who look and live like the viewer.
- Show numbers and momentum: "40,000 guys are switching", comment screenshots, a full inbox. The ongoing "-ing" form feels like a wave.
- *From: Influence, Halbert.*

### 27. Be liked
- People buy from people they like: similar, on their side, familiar.
- Say the similarity out loud ("I'm 22, I was waking up at noon too"). Stand with the viewer against a common enemy.
- Pair the product with winners and good feelings. A recurring face, format and catchphrase builds familiarity.
- *From: Influence.*

### 28. Show authority
- Titles, clothes and settings work as shortcuts. Use the creator's real credential, a relevant expert or a fitting setting, in one line or one shot.
- *From: Influence.*

### 29. Use scarcity, for real
- Rare or disappearing things are wanted more, especially newly scarce ones and ones other people want.
- Exclusive information hits hardest: "most people never hear this".
- Use what's actually limited: a deadline, spots, a price going up, a sellout. Show demand.
- *From: Influence, Predictably Irrational.*

### 30. Give a reason ("because")
- Any ask with a reason gets more yeses, even a thin reason. Every offer, deadline and CTA gets a "because".
- *From: Influence, Great Leads.*

### 31. Choose the emotion: hope leads
- One real stake, then hope, which is far stronger than fear.
- Pure greed attracts flaky, refund-heavy buyers. People want success and more money, but they don't see themselves as greedy, so frame money as freedom, proof or security.
- Head and heart together: pure emotion reads as pandering.
- *From: Masterson.*

## F. Offer and CTA

### 32. Build the offer
- "Free" beats almost free: "first week free", not "$0.99". Real zeros work as claims too ("zero fees").
- Use a decoy. A slightly worse option at the same price makes the target the obvious pick.
- Don't lead with a discount on a product people judge by how it feels (health, focus, sleep). Price signals quality. Lead with full value and reveal the deal as a reward.
- Trials and guarantees create owners; cancelling then feels like a loss. A real "going away" pulls hard.
- Tease what's inside and give it fresh names; never hand over the secret itself.
- Pick for the viewer when options are close ("most people should start with...").
- *From: Predictably Irrational, Great Leads, Take Their Money.*

### 33. Make the ask
- Ask clearly and with confidence; a soft ask reads as doubt. Ask one person for one specific action ("comment SOLIS", "tap the link and set your alarm"), not a vague "check it out".
- Pair a direct ask (buy, download, use the code) with a low-risk one for the not-ready (comment a keyword, follow for part 2, the free guide).
- Organic asks are favors. Don't attach a tiny reward; it kills them.
- Give a reason to act now, and close with a choice: the new life, or back to how it was.
- *From: StoryBrand, Influence, Predictably Irrational, Take Their Money.*

## G. Writing and editing

### 34. Write clearly
- Spoken lines at a grade 4-6 reading level: short, active and concrete. Simple lines are believed more.
- One idea per line. Contract everything ("it's", not "it is"). Slang and fragments are fine if a person would say them.
- Complicated writing often hides weak thinking.
- *From: Masterson, Halbert.*

### 35. Run the edit pass
Before a script ships, go start to finish once:
- Read it out loud. If the creator would stumble, rewrite it.
- The "so what" test: cut any line a busy stranger wouldn't care about.
- Split long lines at "and" or "because". Swap vague pronouns ("it", "they") for vivid names. Cut extra "that"s, hedges ("may", "could", "some") and lazy "-ly" words. Swap big words, repeated words, and words people secretly hate ("learn", "work" become "the trick", "I'll show you").
- Explain jargon in the same breath. Turn flat words into strong ones ("upset" becomes "livid").
- *From: Halbert.*

### 36. Use on-screen text and emphasis
- On-screen text carries skimmers and people watching with the sound off: two to five words, a benefit the hook didn't give, placed just before the line that proves it.
- Scale emphasis to the news. Save the loudest treatment (caps, color, size, motion) for the biggest moment; emphasize everything and it's just yelling.
- Captions: short lines, first line carries a benefit, no wall of text.
- *From: Halbert.*

### 37. Run the bag audit
- A buyer won't decide until every reason is answered. Check the script for: the problem, the solution, who it's for, the trigger to act now, the main objection, proof from people like them, risk reversal and why this one beats the others.
- In 30 seconds each can be one line, one shot or one piece of on-screen text, but a missing one shows up as "I'll think about it".
- Name the missing piece instead of saying "it feels off".
- *From: Brain Audit.*

## H. Breaking down a winner

### 38. Read the subtext
- For each line of a winning ad, name what it does underneath: which emotion (new, easy, safe, big), which lever (contrast, give first, small yes, people like them, liking, authority, scarcity, a reason, anchor, free, hot state), which story beat (want, villain, guide, plan, ask, stakes, success).
- Name its opening type and how direct it is. That shows which awareness stage it's aimed at.
- Note which bags it skips; that's what this audience doesn't need to hear.
- Borrow the structure underneath, never the words. An idea lifted whole loses its power.
- *From: Take Their Money, Great Leads, Brain Audit, Masterson.*
