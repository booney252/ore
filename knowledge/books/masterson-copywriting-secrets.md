# Skill tree: Copywriting Secrets of the Masters (Michael Masterson)

Ore's notes on the book's ideas (a collection of Masterson's essays), in our own words, and how they apply to short-form video copy. The essays on productivity, careers and the AWAI sales pitches are left out.

## Root: good writing is compelling thoughts, expressed clearly

- That's the whole definition. Two parts, both required.
- **A compelling thought** makes the reader think "that's interesting", "I never thought of that", "I have to remember this". It engages the head and the heart at once (people don't buy on emotion alone; pure emotion reads as pandering). Its job is the "ah-ha" moment.
- **Clear expression**: the easier something is to understand, the more likely it is to be believed. Simple statements earn more trust than complex ones that mean the same thing (cognitive fluency).
- **Where compelling ideas come from**: research. Read deep and wide until something gives you the ah-ha. Copying another ad's idea doesn't work; an idea lifted whole loses most of its power. Borrow structure, not ideas.

## Branch 1: The Big Idea and the Rule of One

### 1.1 One idea per piece
- Readers rate pieces that present one idea highest. The great headlines are almost all built on a single big idea ("They laughed when I sat down at the piano...", "The secret of making people like you").
- Weak copy throws every feature and benefit in, hoping one sticks. That's B-level copy: the points compete and pull attention in different directions.
- **Five "ones" of a great piece**: one good idea, one core emotion, one captivating story, one desirable benefit, one inevitable response (one clear next step).

### 1.2 Find the idea behind the idea
- An assertion isn't a compelling idea ("this company is buying smaller ones" is an assertion). Make it **more universal and more unique**: name the pattern ("Sharks: companies that beat the market by eating small, profitable ones"), then prove the pattern, then apply it.
- **Dinner-party test**: could the viewer repeat the idea in one phrase tonight and start a conversation?
- **One-sentence test**: write the main idea in one sentence above the script. If it takes a paragraph, there's no idea yet.

### 1.3 Put the best idea first
- The compelling thought goes in the lead, not later. Don't save the best for last; hit first with the best, and spend the rest proving it.

## Branch 2: Proof

After the compelling thought, the reader's skepticism kicks in ("before I act on this, does it make sense?"). Prove every claim and promise. Three kinds:
- **Factual proof**: research, numbers, studies, sources.
- **Anecdotal proof**: stories that show instead of tell. Very powerful, because people stop being critical when they're being entertained.
- **Social proof**: testimonials, expert endorsements, what others like them are doing.

## Branch 3: Headlines (hooks)

### 3.1 Decode a winner's DNA; don't copy its words
- Study classic and current winners from the inside: what's happening beneath the surface, which triggers fire in the reader's head and heart. That deep structure is the template, invisible to everyone else looking at the same ad. Never reuse a living writer's words.

### 3.2 The underdog structure ("They laughed when I sat down at the piano... but when I started to play!")
- Fifteen words deliver one idea, one benefit, one emotion, one solution, and a whole story.
- The structure: an ordinary person like the viewer tries something extraordinary; people doubt or mock them; they prove everyone wrong. It taps two of the deepest desires: **approval and vindication**.
- It works across products (an investment system the experts doubted; a cheap product the snobs mocked until they tried it).

### 3.3 Short headlines usually win
- Most top headlines are eight words or fewer: easy to take in, impossible to miss. Use the deck (in a video: the on-screen text, the caption's first line) to build on the hook.
- A short hook still has to link to the big idea, compel, and imply a benefit.
- **Breakthrough hooks are tipping points**: they put into words what a group already thinks, feels and fears about something right now ("The Plague of the Black Debt" named a live anxiety with one metaphor).

## Branch 4: Leads (the first seconds)

### 4.1 The lead is the 10% that decides 90%
- The first 300-500 words of a letter (in a video: the first few seconds after the hook) decide whether anything after them gets read.

### 4.2 The problem/solution lead, told as a story
- Don't state the problem in the abstract. Put a hero (ideally the creator) in the problem, and **start in the middle of the conflict** ("Having survived four layoffs, every day at work felt like a gamble").
- Make it visible: a place and a time. Raise the stakes (it gets worse). Then show the solution.
- Keep the climb short: skip the slow details so success feels fast.
- **Plant a named secret** (a system with a name) just as the viewer thinks they know where this is going. It reopens curiosity.
- Give real value inside the piece: the viewer feels obliged to give back (reciprocity) and you can answer an objection at the same time.
- End by repeating the original promise.

### 4.3 Story leads
- Nobody can resist a good story. A story sneaks in sideways: it delivers proof and promise before the viewer realizes they're being sold.
- Storytelling rules: start in the middle, conflict (stated or implied), a hero the viewer identifies with, an emotionally satisfying payoff.

### 4.4 Hook a deeper desire
- Go one level below the obvious promise (not just "six-figure income" but "the career you actually want"). Rhymes help memory ("from layoff to payoff"). A small, specific number of steps ("3 steps") feels doable.

## Branch 5: The Golden Thread and the core complex

### 5.1 The Golden Thread
- The product is at one end of a thread and the prospect's heart is at the other. Every element must connect to both, and the thread has to stay taut. If it goes slack for a second, you lose the sale.
- **Don't forget the product.** An entertaining ad that leaves people unsure what was advertised (the Dos Equis man could be drinking any beer) fails where the Hathaway shirt ad won: the product was part of the story.
- Link the feeling the hook creates to the feeling needed at the close.

### 5.2 The core complex
- The core complex is **one subtle feeling plus one tipping-point idea** that together create the urge to buy.
- Ask what the customer really wants, and don't stop at the first or second answer. The real motive is often social: the "most interesting man" is about being more interesting than other men, not about women; women often dress to impress other women, not men.
- If drafts keep needing rewrites, the problem is usually that the core complex hasn't been found. Go back to research.

### 5.3 Story appeal
- Put the product in a story with a little fiction, romance or intrigue (the Hathaway man with the eye patch). A small, unexpected visual detail can make a whole campaign.

## Branch 6: Emotion choice: hope over fear, never greed

- **Fear and greed are overrated.** Fear's natural responses are fight, flight or freeze, and too much of it lowers response. Lead with fear only for protection products (insurance, alarms, monitors, backups). Everywhere else: one beat of a real stake, then **hope**, which is far stronger.
- **Greed attracts the worst buyers**: gullible, refund-heavy, gone after one purchase. Good customers want success and more money, but they don't see themselves as greedy.
- Better levers (Cialdini): **reciprocity** (give first), **commitment and consistency** (a small yes first), **social proof** (others like them), **liking** (a creator who feels like a friend).
- **Courage and empathy are the marketer's two virtues** (ego and laziness are the two vices). Empathy finds what the customer really feels; courage puts it in an ad. Bold ads offend some people; that's acceptable when they bring in more than they lose, but the point is never to offend, it's to show the customer you get them.
- **Business is like love, not war.** Don't bully, badger or trick the buyer. Aim for a relationship that lasts, because that's what makes a business last.

## Branch 7: Clarity

- Target a reading level around grade 7-8 (Flesch-Kincaid 8.5 or lower). Simple writing gets read and believed; writers with the simplest prose had the best renewal rates. Spoken scripts should be simpler still (natural dialogue sits around grade 4-6).
- **How to get there**:
  1. One idea at a time; cut related-but-separate ideas.
  2. Open with a story: concrete, active sentences.
  3. Avoid complex sentences joined by "whereas", "therefore", "notwithstanding".
  4. Active voice ("John kicked the ball").
  5. Handle objections through dialogue (low reading level, and it brings the idea to life).
- Complicated writing often hides weak thinking.

## Branch 8: Length

- **Need-to-know products** (people already need them) sell fast: state what's unique and make the offer irresistible.
- **Want-to-know products** (people have to be made to want them: courses, info, self-improvement) need more room to build desire, prove it and answer objections. People say they hate long copy, but they respond to it. The job isn't to fit everyone; it's to fully sell the one in a hundred who stops.
- For video: a need-to-know product can be a 15-second ad. A want-to-know offer (a course, a new method) usually needs a longer piece, a series, or a longer funnel step after the hook.

## Branch 9: Practice and testing

- **There's no perfect copy.** The market judges. Get copy to very good and test it. Rewriting forever usually means the core complex is missing, or fear of criticism.
- **Peer review** gets as close to the market as you can before testing.
- **Practice beats reading about it**: study one winner a day (what's it trying to do, how well, what else could work), then write one small ad a day from an alternative approach. Humility makes you better; pride stalls you.

## How Ore uses this
- Every script starts with a one-sentence big idea (the dinner-party test), one core emotion, one story, one benefit and one next step.
- Hooks are short, name a feeling the audience already has right now, and link to the product. The underdog structure (doubted → proved them wrong) is a go-to story shape.
- The first seconds start mid-conflict, raise the stakes, then turn to the solution and plant a named secret.
- Every claim gets factual, anecdotal or social proof.
- The product stays in the story start to finish (the Golden Thread).
- Hope over fear, never greed, and at least one Cialdini lever (give first, a small yes, others like them, a likable creator).
- Spoken lines read at grade 4-6: short, active, concrete; objections handled as dialogue.
