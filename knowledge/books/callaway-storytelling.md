# Skill tree: 6 storytelling techniques (Callaway, YouTube)

Ore's notes on a short-form creator's video about storytelling, in our own words. He credits each idea to someone else (South Park's creators, Gary Provost, Steve Jobs, Christopher Nolan); the summary below is how they apply to a 15-180s UGC script. Optional tools for story-driven content, not rules: they live only in skill 13 of `knowledge/copywriting-skills.md`, marked optional. Hook rules (skill 10) are unchanged.

## 1. The dance: context, then conflict
- Every story that holds attention alternates: a bit of context (the character is doing something), then a conflict (something goes wrong), then more context (they get past it), then another conflict.
- Conflict opens a loop in the viewer's head; context closes it. That back and forth is what keeps them watching.
- The tactical test (from South Park's writers): between every two beats of the outline, the joining word should be **"but"** or **"therefore"**, never **"and then"**. "And then" beats just pile on detail, and the viewer drifts off.
- His example opened with four "but" turns in 30 seconds: the thing is huge, but how did it happen, it was about to be discontinued, but a group of moms changed it, and it gets even better...

## 2. Rhythm: vary the sentence length
- Sentences of the same length read like a stuck record. The ear wants variety. Short. Medium length. And then, once the listener is settled, a long one that builds and builds toward the moment that matters.
- Check: put each sentence on its own line. The right edge should look jagged. If every line ends at about the same place, the script will feel monotonous and people will swipe.

## 3. Tone: talk to one friend
- The most watched creators sound like they're in the room with you, not presenting. When it feels like a conversation, the viewer stops asking "am I being sold to?"
- Write the script as if you're texting or sending a voice note to one close friend. Film as if you're talking to them (some creators tape a photo of a friend under the lens).

## 4. Direction: write the ending first
- Decide the last line before anything else, then work backwards. Make it so memorable that it's the line someone would repeat to a friend.
- Short-form videos loop, so the last line is also the setup for the first line on the replay.
- Practical method: write the first line and the last line, leave space, then fill in the middle with the dance.

## 5. Story lenses: your angle, not the topic
- A topic everyone covers looks the same through everyone's eyes. A lens is your unusual angle on it.
- Rank the angles: the most common one (what everyone says), a less common one, and the least common one. Pick the least common angle you can still tell well, so the video is a category of one.

## 6. The hook
- The first line should say what the video is about as punchily as possible. Avoid vague openers like "wait till you see this" or "you won't believe this": if the first line doesn't grab right away, the video is dead.
- Visual hooks beat audio-only hooks by a wide margin: eyes are faster than ears. Show something on screen that confirms what you're saying from the first frame, instead of only a talking mouth and captions.
- In short: get to the point, and show while you tell.
