# Skill tree: Great Leads (Michael Masterson and John Forde)

Ore's notes on the book's ideas, in our own words, and how they apply to short-form video copy. The Rule of One basics and the problem/solution lead told as a story are in `masterson-copywriting-secrets.md`; this file adds only what's new. Source: the Portuguese edition (intro to chapter 4 read in full; the chapters on the six lead types after the offer lead are summarized from the book's framework).

## Root: the lead decides the sale, and what the viewer already knows decides the lead

- The lead is the first 100-600 words of a long ad. In a video it's the hook plus the first 3-10 seconds. Roughly 80% of a piece's emotional impact comes from its first 20%. Same body, different lead: response often moves 2-3x.
- Direct response has two jobs: move the viewer emotionally, then persuade them logically. The emotional part comes first, and it has to happen in the lead. A viewer who feels nothing in the first seconds never reaches the logic.
- **The prediction test**: read only the lead and ask "does this excite me and make me need to keep going?" If yes, the piece will probably work.
- **The one question**: before writing, ask "what does the viewer already know?" About us, about the product, about their own problem, about other fixes. That answer decides what to say, how to say it and when.
- Big-idea filter (adds to the Rule of One): the idea must be big enough to matter, easy to grasp, instantly compelling, clearly about the viewer, and **easy to believe**. The last one is the one writers skip.

## Branch 1: Awareness picks the lead

### 1.1 The five stages, read as "what the lead must do"
Same five stages as `rmbc.md`. What changes is the job of the opening:
- **Most aware**: they know us and want it. Just give the deal. No education needed; something new or a good offer is enough.
- **Product aware**: they know what we sell but aren't sure it's best for them. They read reviews. The lead must build trust fast and prove we do what we say. Don't spook them.
- **Solution aware**: they know the result they want and that fixes exist, but not that ours delivers it. Show we understand what they want, then show we get them there, with more claims and proof than a plain offer.
- **Problem aware**: they only have worry, no hope yet. Show you feel their pain before any benefit or product. Find the **point of maximum anxiety** (the exact moment the problem hurts most) and name it.
- **Unaware**: they don't know us, the product, or that their problem is worth fixing. The lead must hold attention while giving no sign it's a sales pitch. Push too hard or show the product too early and they're gone.

### 1.2 How to place a market
- Play detective. How new is the product? Is there anything like it? Did they buy before? A brand-new category means low awareness; an existing category means they already have a reference point.
- **Over-aware markets** exist too: they've seen every pitch, tried products like ours and been let down. Treat them like a less aware market (indirect leads), because a direct pitch gets filed under "heard it, tried it".
- Growth lives in the lower stages. Most aware buyers are easy but few; problem aware and unaware markets are where new customers come from, and where skill pays.

## Branch 2: Direct or indirect

### 2.1 The rule
- The more aware the viewer, the more direct the lead. The less aware (or more skeptical), the more indirect.
- **Direct** means the product, the offer or the main benefit shows up right away. Works when the product is easy to understand, the promise is easy to accept, the deal is strong or guaranteed, they already trust us, or the market expected this upgrade.
- **Indirect** means the opening is a story, a piece of news or a strange fact, with no product claim yet. Works when they know little about us, they're jaded and skeptical, there's a news hook too big to ignore, or we're reinventing the product or the idea behind it.

### 2.2 Why indirect wins big when it wins
- Skeptical viewers resist anything that looks like an ad. Indirect openings slip past that, the way a smaller army wins with surprise instead of a frontal charge.
- A direct lead hands the viewer a conclusion. An indirect lead lets them reach it themselves, and a conclusion they reach themselves sticks. That's why indirect leads take more work: you have to know the viewer better than they know themselves.

### 2.3 Indirect lead risks
- Going indirect when direct would do.
- Drifting so far that the bridge to the product breaks.
- Taking too long to reach the product, and boring them.
- Writing something interesting but irrelevant. Clever is not the same as indirect; many funny ads never make clear what they sell.

## Branch 3: The six lead types (most direct to most indirect)

### 3.1 Offer lead (most aware)
- Open on the deal: price, discount, free trial, bonus, guarantee. The easiest lead to write; a strong offer to a ready audience almost sells itself.
- **Formula**: (1) lead with the offer; (2) pick its most emotional detail (often the guarantee, not the price); (3) stay on that one offer and benefit; (4) give a **reason why** (stock is low, a deadline, an event, a launch). The reason is the step writers forget.
- Answer "what's the catch?" before they ask. If there is a catch, say it and make it a plus (limited spots, who gets in).
- The deal must feel like a steal. That can be a low price, a big guarantee, a **bonus big enough to melt resistance**, or even a high price when exclusivity is the draw.
- Variant: the **invitation** ("you've been picked", "we're looking for people who..."), strong for exclusive or long-awaited offers.

### 3.2 Promise lead (product and solution aware)
- The most common lead. One step less direct: the product may wait, but the strongest benefit comes first, often in the hook, the first line and the close.
- The promise has to be big, specific and believable at once. Back it within seconds with a credibility line or proof, or the bigness turns into doubt.
- Amplify one promise instead of listing several. Pair it with a guarantee or a demonstration when the market has heard similar claims.

### 3.3 Problem-solution lead (solution and problem aware)
- Already covered in our Masterson notes. What this book adds: open on the viewer's hottest emotional issue, not the product, and follow it fast with the promise. Empathy first ("I get it"), then hope.

### 3.4 Big secret lead (problem and solution aware)
- Tease exclusive knowledge: a hidden fix, a hidden cause, or a system. The magician's velvet bag: show that something valuable is inside, never open it in the lead.
- Rules: name the secret (a label makes it real); tie it to a clear benefit; hint at why it works; reveal it only after the purchase or click. Give the secret away and the reason to act is gone.
- Strong where the viewer has tried things and thinks nothing works: a secret explains why the others failed (this is where UMP and UMS live).

### 3.5 Proclamation lead (unaware, skeptical)
- A bold statement, prediction or shocking fact that knocks the viewer off balance ("what never to eat on a plane"). Its job is to disarm them long enough to start the argument.
- The closer to unbelievable while still true, the better. It must be proved right after, and bridged to the product before attention fades.
- Works best on news or a trend the viewer half senses (naming a fear with one strong phrase).

### 3.6 Story lead (unaware, the most indirect)
- Everyone listens to a story, including people who'd reject a pitch. The book's most famous example is the "two young men" letter: two near-identical graduates meet 25 years later, one is a manager and one is president. The product isn't mentioned in the opening at all.
- Rules: tell it fast, make the hero someone the viewer recognizes as themselves, put a contrast or conflict up front, and tie the story to the core promise early. A testimonial, a guru's biography or a historical track record can all carry a story lead.

## Branch 4: Making the system work

- Pick the lead in order: find the one big idea, place the market on the awareness line, choose direct or indirect, then choose the lead type.
- Real leads blend types (a story that hides a secret; a proclamation that opens a story). Fine, as long as one idea and one emotion rule.
- Every lead type must reach the benefits early and reach the product and the offer before it ends.
- Write several leads for the same piece and test them. The body can stay the same; the lead is where results move.

## How Ore uses this
- **Pick by awareness** (brand.md `target_awareness` or the brief's call): Most aware → offer. Product aware → promise or offer. Solution aware → promise, problem-solution or secret. Problem aware → problem-solution or secret. Unaware → proclamation or story.
- **Direct or indirect**: cold paid traffic and organic posts default to indirect (story, proclamation, secret). Retargeting and own-audience posts go direct (offer, promise).
- **What each looks like in the first 3-10 seconds**:
  - Offer: the deal and the guarantee on screen in second one, then the reason why.
  - Promise: the one big result as the spoken hook, a proof shot or credibility line by second 3.
  - Problem-solution: the creator inside the worst moment of the problem, "I get it", then the turn.
  - Secret: "the thing nobody tells you about X", the secret named but not revealed, the reason it beats what they tried.
  - Proclamation: one bold, true claim or prediction on screen, proof by second 5, bridge to the product by second 10.
  - Story: start mid-scene with a relatable hero and a contrast; the promise lands by second 8-10.
- **Breakdowns** name each winner's lead type and how direct it is. That tells us the awareness level the winner is aimed at.
- **Iterations** can keep a winner's idea and swap its lead type to test a different awareness level.
- **Every lead** answers "what's the catch?" for offers, proves big promises within seconds, and never lets an indirect opening forget the product.
