# Skill tree: The Copywriting Method Part III, the editing formula (Bond Halbert)

Ore's notes on the book's ideas, in our own words, and how they apply to short-form video copy. The book edits print and web sales letters; each rule below is translated for spoken lines, on-screen text and captions. Reading level is covered in the Masterson notes (Clarity), so it isn't repeated here.

## Root: editing builds the greased slide

- Editing can't save a bad offer. The selling power comes from research (hook, offer, big idea). Editing decides how many of the right people reach the CTA.
- **The viewer is always looking for a safe place to stop.** Once they feel they've got the gist, they leave. In a feed, that's a swipe. The edit's job is to never give them that spot.
- Great copy makes them forget they're watching. Anything that makes them rewind or notice the writing breaks the spell.

## Branch 1: How to run the edit

### 1.1 Write fast, edit slow
- The first draft is a quick, rough dump. Don't edit while writing; it kills the flow of ideas.
- The less unique the offer, the more the edit matters. But never polish so long that it delays testing new hooks.

### 1.2 Edit in complete passes
- Go start to finish every pass. Fixing the opening twenty times and the ending once makes a script that falls apart at the CTA.
- Mood changes copy: a bold day punches it up, a nervous day tones it down. Passes on different days, with rest between, even out the energy: as exciting as possible without promising more than the product delivers.

### 1.3 Read it out loud
- Silent rereading skips the hiccups. Aloud, every rough spot is obvious. If the creator would stumble, rewrite it.
- Grammar isn't the goal. Slang, fragments and made-up words are fine if they sound like a person talking.

## Branch 2: Make it look easy to start

### 2.1 Eye relief
- A wall of text looks like work and gets skipped. In video: short lines, a cut or a new beat every line or two, on-screen text in a few words (never a paragraph), captions in a readable font with strong contrast, checked on a phone the way the viewer sees it.
- Brevity means no wandering, not short scripts. Restating a benefit three ways beats taking forever to reach it. Contract everything ("it's", not "it is").

### 2.2 Subheads become on-screen callouts
- Some viewers watch it all, some skim, some decide in a glance. A subhead hooks the skimmer with a benefit the hook didn't mention.
- In video: on-screen callouts carry the skimmer and the sound-off viewer. Rank the benefits not in the hook, cut each to two to five words ("Takes one afternoon"), and place it just before the line that proves it.

## Branch 3: Clarity hunts

### 3.1 "And" and "because" hunt
- Most long sentences split at "and" or "because" into two stronger statements. A line can be too long, never too short.

### 3.2 Pronoun hunt
- "He", "it", "they", "this one" are clear to the writer and lost on the viewer, who can't stop the video to ask. Replace them with the real name or, better, a strong description from a prepared list (not "the app" but "the alarm that won't let you snooze").

### 3.3 "That" hunt
- About nine in ten uses of "that" can be cut or the sentence rebuilt without it. Keep the few that are needed.

### 3.4 Big word and repeat word hunt
- Swap any word the viewer might not know for a common one. Nobody buys from someone who makes them feel stupid.
- Don't use the same word close together, and never the same verb twice in one script. Rotate synonyms.

### 3.5 Jargon, used right
- A technical term shows you know the field. Say it, then explain it in plain words in the same breath. Spell out every acronym.

## Branch 4: Keep them watching

### 4.1 The anchor
- Promise something valuable later ("in a second I'll show you...") and delay it until they're hooked. Optional when the script already keeps rising.

### 4.2 Three tools, mixed
- **Cliffhanger:** says outright that more is coming ("and then it got worse", "here's the part I regret").
- **Incomplete thought:** a complete sentence that leaves the story open, so the viewer wonders what happened or why ("When the results came in, I was crushed.").
- **High note:** a line so useful the viewer thinks "good idea" and wants the next one.
- Mix all three. Too much of one gets cheesy; a good mix goes unnoticed.

### 4.3 How to add a cliffhanger
- Tack a clause onto the line before that hints at what's next ("...and here's why that's cheap").
- Sum up the last point, then give only half of the next one.
- End a line on "but..." or "so..." (sparingly). Swap a few "but"s for "yet" so it doesn't sound like arguing.
- Where the script jumps between unrelated points, bridge it: "Anyway", "Plus", "Here's the thing".

### 4.4 Punch at the end
- Put the exciting part last in the line, like a comedian's punchline. The last words are what pull them into the next beat.

## Branch 5: Punch it up

### 5.1 Emphasis, scaled to the news
- Emphasis scales with how good the news is. In video the ladder runs from voice tone to on-screen text, caps, color, size, and motion that stops fast. Only the best news gets the loudest treatment; emphasize everything and it's just yelling.

### 5.2 Your own power words
- Don't borrow another writer's list; it sounds forced. Build two lists from tabloid headlines and the audience's own words, positive ("jaw-dropping") and negative ("crisis"), plus phrases. Swap flat words on the edit ("upset" becomes "livid", "rare" becomes "impossible to find").

### 5.3 Qualifier and adverb hunt
- Cut "some", "may", "could", "possibly", "mostly" unless they make the line true. "This may work" and "This works" are miles apart.
- Cut lazy intensifiers ("very", "extremely", most "-ly" words) or replace them with an image ("the room looked like a hurricane hit it"). Keep the few that add punch.

### 5.4 "I" to "you"
- Viewers hate bragging and love a struggle. Talk about yourself in the low moments. When it turns good, the win becomes the viewer's: luck you spotted or hard work they get to skip.

### 5.5 Move answers up
- Answer each doubt just before it forms: credentials before the claim, "it's simple" before it sounds hard, the guarantee before the price.

### 5.6 The "so what" test
- Watch the draft as a busy, selfish stranger. Cut every line that earns a "so what?" (backstory, how you decided, credentials that don't matter yet).

### 5.7 Words people secretly hate
- People want to play guitar, not learn guitar; easy money, not work. Swap "learn", "teach", "study", "work" for "the trick", "the formula", "I'll show you". Check against the target audience: hated words differ by group.

### 5.8 Present progressive
- Ongoing action feels like movement and FOMO. "Thousands are switching" beats "thousands switched". Keep stories in the past tense.

## How Ore uses this
Before a script ships, Ore runs one full pass, start to finish:
- **Out loud:** every spoken line reads smoothly, with no stumbles and no rewinding.
- **So what:** cut every line a busy stranger wouldn't care about.
- **Move answers up:** each likely doubt is answered just before it forms.
- **Hunts:** split long lines; vivid names for vague pronouns; cut extra "that"s, qualifiers and lazy "-ly" words; swap big, repeated or secretly hated words; explain jargon at once.
- **"I" to "you":** the creator's struggle is theirs, the win is the viewer's.
- **Punch:** each line ends on its strongest word. Cliffhangers, open-ended lines and high notes are mixed, so there's no safe place to swipe. Flat words become power words; present progressive for momentum.
- **On-screen text:** two to five words, a benefit the hook didn't give, placed before the line that proves it, readable on a phone; the loudest emphasis only on the biggest moment.
- **Captions:** short lines, the first line carries a benefit, no wall of text.
