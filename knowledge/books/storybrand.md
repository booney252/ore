# Skill tree: Building a StoryBrand (Donald Miller)

Ore's notes on the book's ideas, in our own words, and how they apply to short-form video copy. The book is about websites, pitches and emails; the chapter on company culture is left out.

## Root: the viewer is the hero, the creator or brand is the guide

- People scan for what helps them survive and thrive (money, time, status, belonging, health, meaning). Anything else is noise, and the brain tunes it out. **If you confuse, you lose.**
- Story is how the brain sorts information without effort. Nearly every story runs one plot: a **character** who wants something hits a **problem**, meets a **guide** who gives them a **plan** and **calls them to action**, which helps them **avoid failure** and ends in **success**.
- **Never make the brand the hero.** A brand that brags competes with the viewer for the lead role. A creator's own past struggle is fine, but only as proof they've been where the viewer is.
- **Three questions** the viewer must be able to answer at any moment: what do I want, what's in my way, what does life look like if I get it (or don't)?
- **Five-second test:** could someone grunt what this is, how it helps them, and what to do next? If not, cut.

## Branch 1: The character wants something

### 1.1 Name one want, tied to survival
- Naming the want opens a gap the viewer needs closed: can this get me there? Pick **one** want per piece; several wants cancel out.
- Make it concrete and tied to survival (money, time, status, belonging, health, meaning). "Become the leader your team loves" lands; "inhale knowledge, exhale success" makes them decode it.

## Branch 2: The problem has a villain and three levels

### 2.1 The villain
- A villain gives the frustration a face. It needn't be a person, but give it personality (the phone that steals your mornings).
- A good villain is a **root cause** (not a feeling: frustration is what the villain does to you), **relatable** (the viewer already hates it), **singular** (one per piece) and **real** (never invent a threat).
- The more the villain is attacked, the more the viewer wants the weapon.

### 2.2 External, internal, philosophical
- **External:** the visible, physical problem (no time to work out, bad coffee at home).
- **Internal:** how that problem makes them feel (embarrassed, intimidated, like a failure). **Brands sell fixes to external problems; people buy fixes to internal ones.** CarMax sells not being worked over by a salesman, not cars.
- **Philosophical:** why it's simply wrong, in "should" or "ought" words ("You shouldn't need to be a barista to make great coffee at home").
- The strongest pitch resolves all three in one move. In a 30s script that can be three lines: the villain causes the external problem, the internal line says how it feels, the philosophical line says why it's not fair.

## Branch 3: The guide shows empathy and authority

### 3.1 Empathy
- People trust whoever understands them. Say out loud that you get the internal problem: "I know what it's like to...", "Nobody should have to...", "I was so sick of..."
- Commonality is a shortcut: when the viewer sees someone like them, they fill the gaps with trust.

### 3.2 Authority
- Empathy without competence is "me too!" from a nutritionist. The viewer needs to see the guide has helped others win.
- Show it, don't brag: a short testimonial, one number, a result on screen, a press logo. One or two beats; stacking proof makes the guide the hero again.
- Empathy answers "can I trust you?" Authority answers "can I respect you?"

## Branch 4: The plan

### 4.1 The process plan
- Buying feels like crossing a fast creek. The plan puts stones across it; even obvious steps reduce fear.
- Three steps is ideal, never more than six: "Download it. Set your wake time. Wake up without the scroll." Steps can be before buying, after buying, or both.
- Give the plan a name ("the 3-minute morning reset"). A named plan feels like more value.

### 4.2 The agreement plan
- A few promises that answer fears: no haggling, cancel any time, no showing your face.

## Branch 5: The call to action

### 5.1 Ask clearly, and keep asking
- Heroes only act when challenged. A soft or hidden ask reads as doubt in your own product. Almost nobody oversells; most asks are whispers.
- **Direct CTA** ("will you marry me?"): download, buy, book, use code X, tap the link.
- **Transitional CTA** ("can I take you on a date?"): low risk, usually free. In short-form that's comment a keyword for the guide, follow for part 2, save this, grab the free checklist, start the free trial.
- Keep the direct ask clear and repeated; the transitional one keeps viewers who aren't ready, creates reciprocity and makes you the guide.

### 5.2 The free offer (the book's lead generator)
- "Join my newsletter" offers nothing. Give real value under a specific title ("5 mistakes people make with their first $10K"). Be generous with the why and much of the how.

## Branch 6: Stakes (avoid failure)

- No stakes, no story. People work harder to avoid a loss than to get a gain, so name the cost of doing nothing.
- **Fear is salt.** Too much and people block it out; none and it's bland. One or two stake lines per script.
- Make the loss concrete: another summer wasted, the house nobody wants to watch the game at, still broke next January.

## Branch 7: Success and transformation

### 7.1 Paint the specific ending
- People want to be taken somewhere. Make it concrete ("a man on the moon", not "a strong space program") and show it: the after shot, the product in happy hands.
- Resolve all three problems: the fix, the feeling, and things now being as they should.
- The three endings people crave: **status** (respect, access, standing), **being made whole** (less stress, less work, more time), **self-realization** (finally proving they could, or accepting themselves).

### 7.2 Identity: from / to
- The deepest motive is to become someone. Ask how the viewer wants friends to describe them, and sell that identity ("passive dog owner" to "every dog's hero").
- The guide ends by telling the hero how far they've come: "You're not the guy who hits snooze five times anymore."

## Branch 8: The marketing roadmap

### 8.1 The one-liner
- A logline for the brand: **character + problem + plan + success.** "Busy moms get a short workout they can actually fit in, so they stay healthy and have energy again." The viewer should think "that's me."
- Repeat it until it feels like too much: bio, caption, a script's last line.

### 8.2 Transformation stories (testimonials)
- "They're great!" says nothing. Ask: what was the problem, how did it feel, what was different, the moment you knew it worked, life now. That arc is a ready-made UGC testimonial script.

### 8.3 Nurture, then ask
- Value posts run problem, plan, life after. About every third or fourth makes a direct offer (problem, product, life after, CTA). Even scrolled-past posts keep you remembered.

## How Ore uses this
- Every script casts the viewer as the hero and the creator or brand as the guide. A creator's own story only shows empathy and authority; it never takes over.
- Every script names one want and one villain, and covers the external, internal and philosophical problem (one line each is enough).
- The guide gets one empathy line and one proof beat, never a pile of credentials.
- Any "how it works" becomes a named plan of 3 steps or fewer.
- Every script ends with a clear direct CTA. Organic posts and cold audiences can pair it with a transitional one (comment a keyword, follow for part 2, free guide).
- Each script has one or two stake lines (the cost of doing nothing) and a concrete success picture, ideally with a from/to identity.
- Each brand gets a one-liner (character, problem, plan, success) for captions and bios.
- Breakdowns map a winner's lines to the seven beats and note which beats it skips.
