# Skill tree: Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion (Robert B. Cialdini)

Ore's notes on the book's ideas, in our own words, and how they apply to short-form video copy. Source: the revised English edition (the Drive file is titled "The Psychology of Persuasion"); the six principles plus the contrast principle and the epilogue.

## Root: click, whirr

- People run on shortcuts. A mother turkey mothers anything that makes the chick sound, even a stuffed predator. Humans have the same kind of trigger features: one cue starts a whole automatic response, no thinking needed.
- The shortcuts are usually right, so people trust them, and the more crowded life gets, the more they lean on them. A feed is as crowded as it gets: a scroller decides in a second on one cue.
- **The word "because" is a trigger.** Asking to skip a copier line with a reason got almost everyone to say yes, even when the reason was empty ("because I have to make copies"). Give every ask a reason.
- **Price is a trigger.** Jewelry that wouldn't sell sold out at double the price: buyers read "expensive" as "good".
- **Jujitsu.** The persuader barely pushes; they line up a force that already exists and let it work, so the buyer feels they decided alone.
- Six forces cover most of what gets people to say yes: reciprocation, commitment and consistency, social proof, liking, authority, scarcity. Plain self-interest (a good deal) is assumed, not a seventh.

## Branch 1: Contrast

### 1.1 What comes first sets the scale
- The same thing feels different depending on what came just before. Lukewarm water feels hot after cold. A $95 sweater feels cheap after a $495 suit. A good house looks great after two run-down ones.
- Show the expensive or painful thing first, then ours. Show the bad "before" first, then the "after". Name the costly alternative (a coach, a clinic, a wasted year) before the price.

## Branch 2: Reciprocation

### 2.1 Give first, and the viewer owes you
- Every culture trains people to repay what they're given. A free soda doubled raffle ticket sales, and it wiped out whether people even liked the giver.
- The gift doesn't have to be asked for, and a small gift can pull a much bigger return.
- For video: give real value inside the post before any ask (the tip, the shortcut, the free tool, the full method). An organic post that teaches something useful earns the follow, the comment or the click.

### 2.2 Rejection then retreat (the concession)
- Ask big, get a no, then step down to the real ask. The step down reads as a concession, and people feel bound to concede back. Volunteers for a zoo trip tripled when they'd first refused a two-year commitment.
- It stacks contrast too, and people who agree this way feel more responsible and satisfied, so they follow through.
- The first ask has to be believable. An absurd opening kills the effect.
- For video: anchor high, then retreat. "I was going to charge $500 for this. Then I made it free" or "You don't need the full program. Just start with this one step."

## Branch 3: Commitment and consistency

### 3.1 People act to match what they've already said or done
- Once someone takes a stand, they defend it. Bettors feel surer of their horse right after paying. A small yes leads to a big yes: homeowners who put up a tiny safety sign later agreed to a giant lawn billboard.
- The small yes changes how people see themselves ("I'm the kind of person who..."). Then they act to fit the new self-image, even on unrelated asks.

### 3.2 What makes a commitment stick
- **Active** (they did something, ideally wrote it), **public** (others saw it), **effortful** (it cost them something), and above all **owned** (they feel they chose it, not because of a big bribe or threat).
- A large reward gets the act but not the belief. A barely sufficient reason gets both.
- Commitments grow their own reasons. Once someone decides, they invent new reasons to support it, and those reasons hold even if the original one is taken away (the car dealer's lowball works this way).

### 3.3 For video
- Get a small yes early: a question the viewer answers in their head ("You've tried waking up at 6 and failed, right?"). Now they're on record.
- Ask for small public acts: comment a keyword, comment their goal, duet, save. A typed comment is active, public and chosen.
- Label the viewer with the identity you want them to live up to ("If you're still watching, you're not like most people who quit").

## Branch 4: Social proof

### 4.1 People decide what's right by watching others
- Canned laughter makes people laugh more, even though everyone knows it's fake.
- Strongest under **uncertainty**: when unsure, people copy. And strongest with **similar others**: a three-year-old learned to swim because another three-year-old could; a lost wallet got returned twice as often when the first finder seemed like "one of us".
- Many people beat one.

### 4.2 For video
- Show people like the viewer doing it (same age, same life, same problem), not models. A creator who looks like the buyer is proof in itself.
- Show numbers of people: "40,000 guys use this every morning", comment screenshots, a stitch of other users, a packed DM inbox.
- Reduce uncertainty for the viewer by showing what others already did, then point at one specific next step. The book's emergency rule ("you, in the blue jacket, call an ambulance") is a CTA rule too: a vague "check it out" lets everyone assume someone else will act.

## Branch 5: Liking

### 5.1 People say yes to people they like
- Tupperware sells through a friend, not the rep.
- What makes people like someone:
  1. **Attractiveness**: a halo; good-looking people seem smarter and more honest, and no one admits it moves them.
  2. **Similarity**: same clothes, same background, same habits, even mirrored speech.
  3. **Compliments**: praise works even when people know it's a tactic, and even when it's false.
  4. **Familiarity**: repeated exposure builds liking, if the contact is pleasant.
  5. **Cooperation**: being on the same team against a shared problem (the salesman who "fights his boss" for your deal).
  6. **Association**: people take on the feeling of whatever they're shown next to (the weatherman blamed for rain, food at fundraisers, a hit song after the station jingle).

### 5.2 For video
- The creator should look and talk like the target viewer, and say so ("I'm 22, I was waking up at noon too").
- Be on the viewer's side against a common enemy (the old way, the overpriced option, the people who said they couldn't).
- Pair the product with good feelings and winners; keep it away from bad news. Praise the viewer ("You're smarter than most people scrolling past this").
- Repetition builds liking: same face, format and catchphrase across posts.

## Branch 6: Authority

### 6.1 People obey the symbols, not just the substance
- Two thirds of ordinary people gave what they thought were max-voltage shocks because a man in a lab coat said to.
- Symbols are enough: **titles** (a man's height grew with his title), **clothes** (a guard uniform or a suit got people to obey strangers and follow a jaywalker), **trappings** (people wait longer before honking at a luxury car).

### 6.2 Credible = expert + trustworthy
- The strongest authority is one who seems to argue against their own interest. A waiter who steers you to a cheaper dish is then trusted on the wine and dessert. Admit a small flaw so the big claims land ("It's not the cheapest. It's the one that works").

### 6.3 For video
- Open with the creator's real credential in one line, or a relevant expert, a lab setting, a scrubs or gym-coach look when it's true for them.
- Use the honest-flaw move once per script.

## Branch 7: Scarcity

### 7.1 Less available means more wanted
- People want what's rare or about to disappear. Loss moves people more than an equal gain ("you're losing $X a month" beats "you could save $X").
- **Reactance**: take away a freedom and people want it more and rate it better. Banned soap seemed to clean better; a banned speech became more convincing unheard; "adults only" made a book more wanted.
- **Exclusive information** persuades most. Buyers told beef would run short bought twice as much; told the news itself was insider info, six times as much.

### 7.2 What makes scarcity hit hardest
- **Newly scarce** beats always scarce (cookies that went from ten to two rated best).
- **Scarce because others want it** beats scarce by accident. Competition is the strongest: a rival buyer turns a fence-sitter into a bidder.

### 7.3 For video
- Frame the cost of not acting as a loss.
- Use what's really limited: a deadline, a spot count, a price going up, a drop that sells out.
- "What they don't want you to know", "most people never hear this" is scarcity of information.
- Show demand: others grabbing it, a sold-out restock, a waitlist.

## The defense (the book's own warning)

- Each lever has a tell: a feeling that you owe them, a knot in the stomach, liking someone too fast, rising arousal under time pressure. The book also says fair use is a partner and faked triggers (canned laughs, actors posing as real customers) make people turn on you hard once spotted. Real proof keeps the shortcut working for us.

## How Ore uses this

- **Breakdowns:** name which levers each winning ad pulls (reciprocity, commitment, social proof, liking, authority, scarcity, contrast) and the exact line or shot that pulls it.
- **Hooks:** lead with one trigger the viewer reacts to before thinking: loss, a similar person, a credential, newly scarce info, or a contrast.
- **Scripts:** give value before the ask. Get a small mental yes early. Show people like the viewer. Put the expensive alternative before the price. Give every ask a "because". Admit one small flaw.
- **CTAs:** one specific action for one person, with a real reason to act now (a deadline, a limit, a loss), and a public micro-commitment for organic (comment a keyword).
- **Creators:** cast and brief for liking and similarity to the buyer; use their real credential and story as authority.
- **Proof:** use real numbers, real users and real scarcity; Ore never fakes them, because a counterfeit trigger burns the trust the shortcut depends on.
