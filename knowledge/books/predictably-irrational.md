# Skill tree: Predictably Irrational (Dan Ariely)

Ore's notes on the book's ideas, in our own words, and how they apply to short-form video copy. Contrast, reciprocity, commitment, social proof, liking, authority and scarcity are covered in `psychology-of-persuasion.md`; this file adds only what Ariely adds.

## Root: the mistakes are predictable

- People make the same errors, the same way, every time, so a script can plan for them.
- Nobody carries an inner price tag. Value comes from what's next to it, the first number seen, and what they expect to feel. The buyer still believes they decided rationally.

## Branch 1: Relativity

### 1.1 The decoy
- People compare what's easy to compare and skip the rest. Add an option that is a slightly worse version of the one you want sold. Nobody buys the decoy, but it makes the target look like the obvious pick. A print-only plan at the same price as print plus web pushed most buyers to the bundle.
- A pricey top tier sells the second-priciest one.
- For video: show three plans where one is a worse copy of the target at the same price ("App alone: $80. App plus coaching: $80.").

### 1.2 Frame the saving against the right number
- $7 off a $25 item feels worth a trip. $7 off a $455 item doesn't. Same $7.
- Quote small savings against a small base, and large ones in dollars, not percent of a big price.

## Branch 2: Anchoring and arbitrary coherence

### 2.1 The first number sticks
- Any number seen while thinking about buying becomes the anchor, even a random one. People whose ID digits were high bid two to three times more for the same products.
- Later prices for related things follow logically from that first, arbitrary number.

### 2.2 Escape the old anchor by being something new
- A product that looks like an old category inherits its price. A coffee chain with new sizes, names and smell escaped the diner's anchor.
- For video: don't let the viewer file you next to the cheap thing they already price. Name the category fresh, then set the anchor.

### 2.3 Self-herding
- People treat their own past choice as proof and repeat it. For video: make the first step tiny and good; it sets the habit.

### 2.4 Pay or be paid
- An unclear experience becomes a treat or a chore depending on which is suggested first. For video: frame effort as a privilege ("you get to"), never a cost.

## Branch 3: The power of FREE

### 3.1 Zero is not a price, it's a feeling
- Free removes the fear of a bad choice, so people overvalue it. Dropping both chocolates by one cent made the free one jump from about a quarter to two thirds of choices, though the gap between them didn't change.
- People take a free small thing over a far better paid deal. Free shipping drove sales; shipping for a few cents did almost nothing.

### 3.2 For video
- Make part of the offer free, not cheap: "free trial", "free shipping", "first week free". Never "$0.99".
- Zero works beyond money: use real zeros as claims ("zero sugar", "no fees").

## Branch 4: Social norms vs market norms

### 4.1 Two worlds that don't mix
- People work hard as a favor or for fair pay. A tiny payment kills effort: people worked harder for nothing than for 50 cents.
- A gift stays social. Name the gift's price and it becomes a payment.
- Once a relationship goes market, it rarely goes back. A brand that sells "we're family" and then charges a harsh fee gets punished like a betrayer.

### 4.2 For video
- Organic asks (comment, share, help a creator) work as favors. Don't attach a small reward to them.
- If you frame the brand as a friend, keep every line consistent with it. Otherwise sell plainly: here's what you get, here's the price.
- Bonuses land as gifts. Don't list their dollar value unless you are deliberately stacking value.

## Branch 5: Arousal (the hot state)

### 5.1 People don't know their hot self
- Aroused, people want and do things they flatly reject when calm, and they can't predict it.
- For video: sell to the hot state. Put the viewer back in the moment of the problem (the 6am alarm, the scale, the empty bank app) before the offer. A calm viewer underrates how badly they want the fix.

## Branch 6: Procrastination and self-control

### 6.1 People know they'll delay, and welcome help
- Given the chance, people set themselves deadlines. Imposed deadlines worked best; no deadlines worked worst.
- Bundling many chores into one simple event stopped delay. Committing future money is easier than spending today's.
- For video: offer the commitment device. "Set it tonight, it locks your phone at 11." Make the product the outside voice. Bundle steps into one tap. Ask for a start date, not a start now.

## Branch 7: Ownership

### 7.1 Owners overvalue
- Ticket winners asked about 14 times what non-winners would pay.
- Effort builds ownership (assembling it yourself). So does imagining it as yours, and trials: after a trial, cancelling feels like a loss.
- For video: get the viewer to picture it as already theirs ("your morning, your stack"). Push trials and guarantees: they feel like a safe test but create owners. Let the viewer set it up or customize it.

## Branch 8: Keeping doors open

### 8.1 People pay to keep options alive
- People burned money keeping vanishing doors alive, even worthless ones. Between two near-equal options they stall, and stalling costs more than either choice.
- For video: a real "going away" (a plan being retired, a price ending) pulls hard. And don't make the viewer choose between near-equal options; pick for them ("most people should start with...").

## Branch 9: Expectations

### 9.1 What they expect is what they taste
- Told beforehand a beer had vinegar, people hated it. Told after, they liked it as much as those never told. Expectation shapes the experience, not just the opinion.
- Nice presentation, rich descriptions and brand names make the same thing taste better (same coffee, fancier containers, higher ratings).
- For video: set the expectation before the demo ("watch how fast this is"). Describe with texture and specifics. Show the product presented well. Save weaknesses for after the experience, never before.

## Branch 10: Price signals quality

### 10.1 The expensive pill works better
- The same placebo relieved pain for almost everyone at $2.50, half at 10 cents. Discounted energy drinks made people do worse on puzzles; full price plus a claim of proof made them do best.
- For video: don't lead with a discount on a product whose value is felt (health, focus, sleep). Lead with full value, then reveal the deal as a reward. Pair the price with a reason to believe it works.

## Branch 11: Honesty and choosing in public

### 11.1 Small cheating
- Most people cheat a little, and getting caught barely matters. A moral reminder just before temptation stopped it. Cheating doubles one step from cash (tokens, points), and brands that cheat in points lose trust. For video: state credits in real dollars.

### 11.2 Ordering out loud
- People choosing in front of others pick to look unique and enjoy it less. For video: offer a choice that signals who they are ("the one serious lifters pick").

## How Ore uses this

- **Breakdowns:** name the Ariely lever a winner uses (decoy, anchor, free, hot state, ownership, expectation, price-quality) and the exact line or shot.
- **Hooks:** open in the hot state, or with an anchor number, or a fresh category so the viewer can't price you as the old thing.
- **Scripts:** set the expectation before the demo. Make the viewer picture owning it. Offer the product as the commitment device. Ask organic favors without tiny rewards.
- **Offers and CTAs:** use a real zero ("free", "first week free"), not a tiny price. Show three options with a decoy that makes the target obvious. Anchor high first. Don't open with a discount on a felt-value product. Pick for the viewer when options are close.
