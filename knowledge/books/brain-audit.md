# Skill tree: The Brain Audit (Sean D'Souza), why customers buy and why they don't

Ore's notes on the book's ideas, in our own words, and how they apply to short-form video copy. Source: an excerpt (intro and Bag 1 in full; Bags 2-7 named only).

## Root: the conveyor belt

A buyer's mind works like an airport baggage belt. They won't leave (decide) until every one of their bags has come off. Leave one reason out and the decision keeps circling: "I'll think about it", fidgeting, then they're gone. When a piece of copy doesn't convert, one bag is missing, and you can find which one.

The seven bags, in the order the mind wants them:

1. **The problem**: what's wrong, made specific and urgent.
2. **The solution**: the way out of that problem.
3. **The target profile**: who exactly this is for (and who it's not for).
4. *The trigger*: the moment that turns "interested" into "now". It sits between the first three bags and the last four.
5. **The main objections**: the doubts that stop them, answered.
6. **The testimonials**: proof from people like them.
7. **The risk reversal**: taking the risk off the buyer.
8. **The uniqueness**: why this one and not the others.

### Skill: the bag audit
- Write first, then audit. Run every script, hook, caption or brief against the seven bags, and name the missing one instead of giving a vague opinion ("feels off").
- A benchmark beats taste: two people auditing against the same bags give the same feedback.
- In a 15-60s video you can't spend long on each bag, but you can't skip one either. The problem (the hook) and the solution carry most of the time. Target profile, objection, proof, risk and uniqueness can each be one line, one on-screen text or one shot.

### Skill: speak the brain's language
- The best offer in the world gets nothing if it's in a language the buyer doesn't speak. Language here means order and framing, not vocabulary: the same offer converts once it's presented in the sequence the brain expects (the bags) and in the buyer's own words for their problem.

## Branch 1: The problem (unlocked)

### 1.1 Problems beat benefits for attention
- The brain is built to spot threats before rewards. Its job is to keep you alive, so it scans for what could go wrong, and a problem grabs attention ahead of anything pleasant. A brain study found people's brains reacted more strongly to problem images than to pleasant ones, and barely at all to neutral ones.
- Most marketing opens on benefits and solutions, and that's why it gets ignored. **Open on the problem.**
- For hooks: the first line names the problem the viewer has (or is about to have), not the product's benefit.

### 1.2 How the brain processes a problem (3 steps)
1. It notices a change ("something's different").
2. It judges how bad it is ("am I in trouble?").
3. It acts to get out of the way.
- Copy has to do all three: make the change visible (pattern interrupt), make the severity felt (consequences), then show the way out (the solution). A problem the viewer doesn't see as severe gets filed as noise.

### 1.3 Elevate the problem: irritant to crisis
- Buyers juggle dozens of problems at once (kids, work, bills). Yours wins only if it jumps to the top of the list.
- Elevating means showing where a small, ignorable irritant leads: a slightly slow laptop becomes "this is how hard drives fail right before a deadline". Same problem, now urgent.
- In scripts, follow the problem line with its consequence line (what happens if they leave it), so it moves from "someday" to "now".

### 1.4 Problems educate; they aren't negativity or scare tactics
- Naming a real problem is like telling a child to look both ways: it's useful information they didn't have. A mechanic pointing out a worn timing belt isn't being negative; the belt will fail, and he's telling you before it costs a fortune.
- The test: is the problem real and is the consequence true? Then saying it is a service, and saying it boldly is fine.
- Often the buyer didn't know the problem existed until you named it. Teaching it turns an unknown into a must-fix.

### 1.5 Problems are relative: a better option creates the problem
- A problem can appear simply because something better exists. Economy class only feels cramped because business class exists; an old car is fine until you're parking it next to everyone else's at work; yesterday's fast computer is slow once a faster one ships.
- Every product is a fix for some problem. If you can't say which one it solves, it has nothing to sell.
- In copy: show the better state (how others are already doing it) and the viewer's current way becomes the problem. "You're still doing X while people like you are doing Y."

### 1.6 Isolate one problem
- Every product solves several problems for several people (a coffee is a break, a meeting, a pick-me-up). A message aimed at all of them lands with none.
- Pick **one** specific problem per piece. "Allergic to cats?" stops the right person; "Got allergies?" stops no one. Specific gets a response because the viewer recognizes themselves.
- It feels like you're throwing away customers. You're not: the specific message pulls more of the right people, and you sell them the rest later.
- For a script: one problem, one person, one piece. Make other problems their own videos.

### 1.7 Isolate by symptom or moment when they don't know the cause
- If the buyer doesn't know the cause of their problem, name the moment they feel it: "Sneeze every night before bed?", "Sneeze after one glass of wine?". The brain recognizes the moment instantly even when it can't name the cause.
- In hooks, name the exact situation (time, place, feeling) the viewer lives through, not the category.

### 1.8 One room at a time (the front door)
- A business is a house with many rooms (products, features, offers). Don't rush a visitor through every room. Bring them in through one front door, one problem and one offer, then show the other rooms once they're inside.
- In a script: sell one thing. Save the rest (other features, other offers, the upsell) for later content or the next step of the funnel.

## Locked: Bags 2-7 (not in the excerpt)

The solution, target profile, trigger, objections, testimonials, risk reversal and uniqueness are named but not explained in the excerpt. They unlock with the full book.

## How Ore uses this
- Every hook isolates one problem (1.6, 1.7), with its consequence (1.3).
- Every script is audited against the seven bags; briefs say which line covers which bag.
- When a winner is broken down, note which bags it covers and which it skips. A winner that skips a bag shows what that audience doesn't need to hear.
