# Winning Ads Report: PeptidePal, 2026-10-25

27 winners in 6 lanes, numbered 1 to 27. Almost all of the long-runners come from adjacent supplement and wellness brands selling to the same 40+ buyer, not from peptide apps. Two direct app ads (Pep AI) and one PeptidePal ad made the list, all in Rising now.

## 1. The big picture

### What runs through everything

**1. "What you tried failed for a reason nobody told you."** (#3, #4, #6, #10, #13, #15, #16, #21, #24)
Every big long-runner names the thing the buyer already tried, then blames the tool and not the buyer. Dulave: "Targets the bacteria your doctor didn't test for." Sculptique: "That's why yours did nothing. You need the dose and the delivery." Kilgour: minoxidil needs an enzyme "up to 70% of menopause or women don't have enough of." Everly's letter: "You weren't taking pumpkin seed oil. You were taking dead oil in a capsule." Purelia: "Why Purelia Works When Milk Thistle, Diet & Specialist Visits Don't." Resilia: "The question was never how do you kill the bacteria. It's how do you break through a shield."
*Why it works on this buyer:* PeptidePal's buyer has already tried Notes, alarms, spreadsheets, ChatGPT and other trackers, and the brief's common thread is that "the user is the glue, and the glue is where the errors happen." These ads show how to call the old way broken without calling the buyer stupid. It's the same two-part shape as PeptidePal's chosen mechanisms (an alarm "only knows *when*, not *whether*"; a system "built for one, not two").

**2. One specific, countable, slightly embarrassing moment.** (#5, #6, #7, #10, #14, #17, #19, #25, #26)
"I no longer know what getting up five times a night means" (#5). "Makes your jeans stop hanging empty in the back" (#6). "You've been in there 40 minutes" (#7). "I had to pee every 90 minutes — even at night" (#19). "Day 7. 2am. You're still asleep" (#26). "My stomach would hurt and swell so bad I would cry in pain" (#17).
*Why it works:* the viewer recognizes their own life in one detail, which is why these ads can run for 6 to 12 months without changing. PeptidePal's buyer has moments just as specific in the brief: "Sunday or Monday?", standing at the fridge counting vials, "I write down what my protein is and then do math", "I just used notes before LOL."

**3. "My doctor brushed me off."** (#3, #4, #13, #14, #15, #16, #21)
"My doctor said drink more water. That was it." (#14). "Your doctor just told you to drink more water" (#13). "My nephrologist said we'd 'keep an eye on it.'" (#21). "Managing it. That word haunts me now." (#21).
*Why it works:* the viewer feels alone with the problem, and the ad takes their side. The brief has the same pain in their words: "I'm on my own now" / "This is why we have to be our own doctors!"

**4. A time ladder: day by day, week by week.** (#2, #5, #10, #11, #12, #14, #15, #21, #26)
"Day 1... Day 3... Day 7... Day 30" (#26). "Week one: 144/98... Week four: 128/82" (#21). "Week two... Week three... Week six" (#10). "Two weeks in, sleeping through the night" (#14). "30 days ago, you would not catch me in this outfit" (#2). Many more in the wider set (Jevawell, Metabolae, IM8, Spoiled Child's month-by-month statics).
*Why it works:* each step is a small payoff, which keeps people watching long videos (76s, 263s), and a slow, modest early step ("Week 1 she didn't notice much" in #10) makes the rest believable. The buyer in the brief wants exactly this: "know exactly where I am this week."

**5. Take the risk away.** (#1, #2, #3, #4, #6, #11, #12, #15, #16, #18, #21, #22, #23, #24)
"Try A Full Size Product Before You Buy" (Spoiled Child, 5 of the 27 ads), 30-day, 60-day and 90-day money-back guarantees, and a guarantee tied to the buyer's own lab numbers (#24).
*Why it works:* these buyers pay cash and have been burned. The brief's objections are full of it: "Bait and switch," "I immediately got charged for $23," "Says free but you need to pay once all your information is loaded in."

**6. Lots of real-looking people, named and aged.** (#1, #2, #11, #12, #15, #17, #19)
"-Becca" (#1). "Jennifer Lawson, 47" (#17). "— Ugo, Age 62" (#19). Seven creators with @handles saying the same result in their own words (#11, #12).
*Why it works:* the brief says this buyer respects people who "show receipts." Many voices saying one thing reads as "this is common," not one person's luck.

**7. A deadline on an ad that never ends.** (#3, #4, #5, #6, #10, #16, #20, #22, #23, #27)
"You have one day" has run for 295 days (#5). "NOW OR NEVER" (#20). "Last sale sold out in 2 days. We have 847 bottles left." (#3, #4, #16). "DOUBLE DISCOUNT END SOON!" (#22, #23). A tariff price hike (#27).
*Why it works:* a reason to act today on something people would otherwise "think about." It mostly sits on supplement offers (bundles, % off). None of the app ads use it.

**8. Look like anything but an ad.** (#7, #10, #13, #15, #21, #24, #25, #26, #27)
A scam warning ("This is fake, this is fake, and this is definitely fake," #13). An honest review ("I received these... as a gift to provide my review on," #25). A black-and-white documentary photo with no text (#21). Farm chores before any product (#27). A doctor answering a viewer's question (#15). "Yes, it's a bribe." (#24). Cartoons for embarrassing topics (#7, #10, #26).
*Why it works:* the brief's objection "This is just another company trying to sell me something" is answered before it forms. Animation in particular lets an ad talk about constipation, odor or 2am bathroom trips without being gross.

**9. The cheap version is the enemy, with a number to prove it.** (#6, #13, #15, #21)
"5,000 milligrams, the full clinical dose, not the gram and a half hiding behind the flavoring" (#6). "Everly uses 3,000 milligrams per serving... The cheap brands on Amazon may be 500 to 1,000 milligrams" (#13). "98% of women in our trial saw results. With menopsy at all that number is around 30 to 40% of best" (#15).
*Why it works:* it gives a reason to pay more than the cheap or free option. For PeptidePal, the "free option" is Notes, alarms and a spreadsheet.

**10. A quiz as the soft next step.** (#1, #2, #11, #12, #15, #22, #23)
"Take the SpoiledBrain™ quiz," "free 60-second scalp age assessment," "answer 4 quick questions to unlock an EXTRA 10% off."
*Why it works:* a small yes instead of "buy now," and it feels personal ("found the product with the right ingredients for me").

**Emotion across all of them:** recognition first (that's me), then a cause that isn't their fault, then relief. Fear is used as a spice (dialysis in #21, "you've already waited too long" in #14), but every ad ends on hope.

### Emerging vs fading

**Emerging (in Rising now and the newer ads, not in the proven ones):**
- **GLP-1 side effects as a market.** Sculptique's rising ad (#6) opens its copy with "Lost weight on GLP-1 but lost your shape with it? That's muscle, not just fat." and has 13 versions in 24 days. Around it in the wider set: Zafira ("It's not laziness. It's your GLP-1."), Jevawell (GLP-1 constipation), Vitawild (GLP-1 electrolytes), Sculptique's "If you're on ozemic, wagovi, or mongaro" (9 versions), Loaded Tea ("Why GLP-1 Users Are Obsessed With This Drink"), Dr. Gordon ("Starting a GLP-1?"). PeptidePal's own rising ad (#7) sits in the same space: constipation, fiber and peak days. *What it means:* the side effects PeptidePal's copy lessons already name (nausea, constipation, protein on peak days, losing muscle) are where supplement money is moving right now. Those brands sell a pill for it. PeptidePal is the only one in the set that can show the peak day coming and the protein number on one screen.
- **Winning scripts copied across categories.** Sculptique's #6 is Everly's #5 with new nouns: "You have one day. If you do not take advantage of this deal..." / "Since taking this, I..." / "Do not miss out on this deal. I'm going to put it right here." It also borrows Everly #13's "don't get scammed... off Amazon" and the milligram comparison. A 295-day script was moved to a new product and is scaling at 2x its brand's normal ad life. The skeleton of a winner is the asset, not the product.
- **Long animated stories with a taboo opening.** Resilia (#10, 263 seconds, 6 versions in 13 days, plus two more Resilia stories in the wider set: "My whole circle knew my husband was cheating" and "Walking on my stepmom..."). Everly's skeleton (#26) and PeptidePal's cartoon skit (#7) use the same idea: animation makes the private problem watchable.
- **Direct app competitors are learning to run.** Both Pep AI ads (#8, #9) are outliving Pep AI's usual 7 days. One is a 40-50 woman listing features over the app; the other is a young man with a celebrity body hook. Neither is a story.

**Fading (in the proven ads, but nobody is launching new versions of it):**
- **Offer-only statics with a medical callout.** Dulave's PPI statics (#3, #4, #16, #17) are 303 to 329 days old, and Dulave's newest ad in the whole set is 252 days old. They still run, but no one is making new ones in this style.
- **Seasonal hooks left running.** "LEAVE THE PUFFINESS IN 2025" (#18), "HOLIDAY SALE IS HERE" (#17), pumpkin emojis on Everly's headlines. They still convert, which says the image does the work and the time peg doesn't matter much.
- **Award title + "try before you buy" + quiz.** Spoiled Child still uses it, but its newest version in the set is 130 days old.
- **Scaled and then stopped:** BBCO's tariff price-lock (#27, 9 versions, stopped 2026-01-02), Innosupps' gut flush, IM8's 30-day drink, Quasi's "Korean secret," Lymphoria's drainage drops, True Nutra's ingredient-character skits. Heavy versioning didn't save them.
- **PeptidePal's own explainer ads.** The half-life and comparison-chart ads ("This is what your Monday dose is still doing on Thursday," "YOUR PROTOCOL: WITHOUT PEPTIDEPAL / WITH PEPTIDEPAL") stopped at 25 to 38 days. The native-looking ones are still alive: the notes-app posts (48 days), the reconstitution cheat card (50 days), the "$300 vial" meme (47 days), Caitlin's car selfie (58 days), "ANOTHER SPREADSHEET" (33 days, 8 versions) and the fiber skit (#7).

### What carries across formats

1. **Testimonial-as-headline (statics #17, #19) works as a UGC first line or an LFS opening.** The biggest text is a customer's own sentence in quotes, with name and age. In video, the creator opens by saying a line like that, word for word, before saying who they are. In LFS, it becomes the first line before "see more."
2. **The day counter (B-roll #26) works as a static and as LFS.** A big "Day N" over each beat turns a long video into a ladder. On a static it becomes a 3- or 4-step timeline (Spoiled Child's month-by-month statics in the wider set already do it). In LFS it's #21's "Week one... Week two... Week four" log, repeated in the P.S.
3. **The scam-warning open (UGC #13) works as a static headline.** "2026 Scam to watch for‼️" plus "This is fake" x3 in video becomes a static that names the fake or cheap option in huge type, the way Zafira's static "We're NOT on Amazon." does in the wider set.
4. **The struck-out problem word (statics #4, #16) works as video on-screen text.** "MUCUS BE GONE" and "PPI TRAP, BE GONE" with a hand-drawn strike are a one-second hook. In video, the first on-screen text can be the problem word with a strike drawn through it while the creator talks.
5. **"Here's how to claim it" (LFS video #22, #23) works for any warm-audience format.** Three numbered steps, one caption and one clip per step, under 12 seconds, with the long copy carrying the proof. Purelia (#24) does the same thing as text ("Here's how the double-stack works: Step 1... Step 2..."). It's a retargeting format, not a cold one.

**Why it's working now:** it stacks three kinds of trust (an award, a peer, no risk) in one frame and asks for almost nothing. For a cash-paying buyer who's been burned by "free" offers, "Full Size" before you pay is the strongest line in the ad.

**Why it's still working:** 361 days on one version. Nothing in it is tied to a moment (the "2025" is the only date and it hasn't hurt). Risk reversal and a quiz don't wear out because every new viewer meets them fresh.

## 3. Gaps

**Pains, desires and objections from the brief that no winning ad touches:**

- **"Did I already take it?" and the second-compound moment.** The brief's biggest pain ("I can't keep it all straight," "how in the world do you keep track of everything?") and its defined buying moment (adding a second compound) appear in none of the 27 winners. The only ads near it are direct apps listing features (#8: "It tracks all your PEPs, your cycle, your schedule"). The winners' favorite move, "a specific, countable moment" (#5, #19, #25, #26), has an exact match in the brief that nobody uses: "Wait, did I take my shot Sunday or Monday?"
- **"I don't trust my own math."** Units, mixing, syringe units: no winner. It shows up only in PeptidePal's own ads (the reconstitution cheat card at 50 days, the "$300 vial" meme at 47 days) and in Peptide Tracker's "10 units." static.
- **Running out mid-cycle.** "my supply tracking was manually adding mgs from looking at the vials in the fridge": no winner. PeptidePal's own vial-countdown slideshow ("I WANT TO KNOW") stopped at 30 days.
- **"I set it all up and the app lost it."** Lost data is the brief's most painful app failure ("ALL my information was deleted like I never put it in"). No ad touches it, including the direct apps.
- **"Every app treats me like a child."** The brief's "I am not five years old!" and "no confetti" are untouched. Worth knowing: the formats rising right now are cartoons (#7, #10, #26), so the brief's dislike is about cartoonish app screens, not ad style.
- **The doctor's office dream.** The winners love the doctor who brushed you off (#13, #14, #15, #21). None of them sells the other half: walking in with a clean history. The brief's "My doctors ask 'what have you been doing?'" and "this time they can show it" have no ad.
- **Price honesty and trial traps.** Every supplement winner leads with risk reversal (try before you buy, 30/60/90-day money back, "Yes, it's a bribe"). No app ad in the set offers anything like it, and the brief's loudest objections are about apps that "start free and then lock things" ("Bait and switch," "I immediately got charged for $23"). Peptide Tracker answers it only with "free."
- **Men, and people under 40.** The winners speak to women 40-70 (Spoiled Child, Kilgour, Dulave's "Jennifer Lawson, 47") and men over 40 (Everly). PeptidePal's real signups are 49% women and 43% men, with 35-44 and 25-34 the two biggest groups. Only Pep AI's young man (#9) speaks to anyone under 35.
- **Information overload.** Caitlin's angle ("the more they research, the less confident they feel") isn't in any of the 27. In the wider set, only Dr. Gordon's ads ("GLP-1 patients get a prescription and no instructions") come near it.

**What the winners touch that PeptidePal's brief owns too:** protein on low-appetite days. Sculptique (#6) sells a powder for "Lost weight on GLP-1 but lost your shape with it? That's muscle, not just fat," and Pep AI (#9) sells "a personalized nutrition plan so you know how much protein to be eating." The pain is being paid for; nobody is showing it as a tracking problem except PeptidePal's own "Nobody told me how important it was to be eating protein on my protocol" (48 days).

**What PeptidePal's own ads do vs competitors:**

- **What's working for PeptidePal:** the native-looking ads. The cartoon fiber skit (#7) is outliving every PeptidePal ad (22 days vs a 7-day norm). "ANOTHER SPREADSHEET" has 8 versions at 33 days. Caitlin's car selfie (58 days), the notes-app posts (48 days), the reconstitution cheat card (50 days) and the "$300 vial" meme (47 days) are all still live.
